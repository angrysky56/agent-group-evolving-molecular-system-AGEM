# Abduction and the Comprehensibility of the World

Einstein was puzzled by the success of natural science. He wrote that the
eternal mystery of the world is its comprehensibility, and thought that we would
never be able to explain it. The fact that the world is ordered in a way our
concepts can capture struck him as a miracle, or at least as something that
ought to remain forever a miracle.

Einstein held that the primary concepts and principles of physics are free
creations of the human mind. They are not abstracted from experience by any
logical route. He described them as freely-chosen conventions, invented rather
than discovered, and defended this against the empiricist view that concepts are
extracted from sense impressions by generalisation.

Yet Einstein also insisted that experience remains the sole criterion of the
physical utility of a mathematical construction. The concepts are free, but
their fate is decided by the world. This tension — free creation constrained by
external verdict — is the heart of the puzzle.

Peirce's account of abduction offers a way of interpreting it. For Peirce,
abduction is the only logical operation which introduces any new idea. It
proceeds from a surprising fact to a hypothesis which, if true, would render the
fact a matter of course. Abduction is very little hampered by logical rules;
it is the process of forming an explanatory hypothesis, and its conclusion is
adopted for testing rather than asserted.

Interpreting Einstein's reflections in this light sheds light on abduction
itself, and we see it applied by a working physicist rather than described by a
logician. Einstein's "free creations" are abductive hypotheses. They are not
derived from experience, which is why they feel free; they are answerable to
experience, which is why they are not arbitrary.

Einstein described pure thinking in terms of playing the violin and smoking a
pipe — activities that are not deliberate reasoning at all. This is precisely
Peirce's point about the context of discovery. The abductive leap is not
governed by rules of inference. Only its subsequent testing is.

If this reading is right, the comprehensibility of the world is less mysterious
than Einstein supposed. It is not that our concepts miraculously match reality.
It is that abduction generates candidate concepts freely and induction discards
the ones that fail. The miracle is relocated, not removed: what remains
unexplained is why the process converges at all.
